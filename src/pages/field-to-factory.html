The process

Six stages, one continuous standard.

Tea making is a sequence, not a single step. What happens early in the process determines what is possible later — and what ends up in the cup.

01

Fresh leaf reception

Freshly harvested leaf is received at the factory and handled promptly. Keeping leaf in good condition from the moment it arrives protects the quality of everything that follows.

02

Withering

Leaf is spread and exposed to controlled air movement, gently reducing moisture and softening the leaf so it can be processed evenly.

03

CTC processing

Crush, tear and curl transforms the withered leaf into a granular style. The result is the bold, quick-infusing character associated with CTC tea.

04

Oxidation

The processed leaf rests under managed conditions while colour, body and flavour develop. Timing and environment shape the character of the finished tea.

05

Drying

Heat is applied to reduce moisture and stabilise the leaf, locking in the qualities developed during processing and preparing it for sorting.

06

Sorting and grading

Finished tea is sorted into commercial grades — BP1, PF1, PD, D1 and F1 — so that buyers can select a leaf style suited to their product and market.

The facility

Manufacturing at the source of the leaf.

Processing where the tea is grown shortens the distance between harvest and manufacture, and keeps responsibility for quality in one place.

Our factory receives fresh leaf from the plantation and carries it through withering, CTC processing, oxidation, drying and grading. The equipment is industrial, the discipline is agricultural: protect what the land and the harvest produced.

This is also what makes a commercial conversation straightforward. When a buyer asks about a grade, the answer comes from the people who processed it.

The finished tea

From production line to commercial grade.

The end of the process is the beginning of the trade relationship. Finished tea is graded, prepared and discussed with buyers in terms of leaf style, cup character and the requirements of their market.

Whether the destination is a bulk blending line, a packing facility or a private-label product, the same principle applies: the grade should match the job it is being asked to do.
